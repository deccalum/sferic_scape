## [1] Few (1969) — "The Acoustics of Thunder"
**Citation:** Few, A. A. (1969). *The acoustics of thunder*. Journal of Geophysical Research, 74(28), 6926–6934.
**DOI:** [10.1029/JC074i028p06926](https://doi.org/10.1029/JC074i028p06926)

### Abstract Summary
A model for the sound generated by a tortuous lightning channel is proposed that describes the principal features of thunder. The model indicates that the power spectrum of thunder should be similar to the power spectrum produced by a short line segment having the same energy-per-unit-length $E_l$ as the most energetic return stroke in the lightning flash.

This model predicts that $E_l$ can be estimated from the frequency of the peak in the power spectrum of thunder $f_m$

$$f_m = 0.63 \times C_0 \times \left(\frac{P_0}{E_l}\right)^{1/2}$$

where $P_0$ is ambient pressure and $C_0$ is the ambient speed of sound.

> **On the constant 0.63:** This is a dimensionless theoretical coefficient derived from cylindrical blast wave (Sedov–Taylor) scaling. It relates the peak-spectrum frequency to the *relaxation radius* of a cylindrical shock — the radius at which the shock transitions from nonlinear to linear propagation. Few derived it analytically from the cylindrical explosion model; it is not empirically fitted.
>
> **code:** `peak_frequency_hz()` in `lightning_model.h`
> <br>Constant `FEW_K = 0.63`.

---
### Key Results

#### Cylindrical Wave Theory
The initial pressure release of a lightning return stroke behaves as a **cylindrical explosive source** (not a point source). This geometry allows $E_l$ to be estimated directly from a measured $f_m$, giving a remote-sensing method for lightning energy.

#### Tortuous Channel Model ("String of Pearls")
Few argued that the long-duration rumble and distinct claps are not reverberation but are intrinsic to the source geometry. A lightning bolt is a kinked, tortuous channel; sound waves originating from different segments and kinks arrive at the observer at different times, producing the extended temporal envelope.

> **code:** `make_channel()` in `lightning_model.h`
> <br>`LightningSegment::delay_s` encodes the per-segment arrival time.

**Sound type taxonomy by observer geometry:**
| Type              | Origin                                                                                  |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Clap**          | Channel segments oriented perpendicular to the observer (maximum projected energy)      |
| **Peal**          | Successive overlapping claps from nearby tortuous segments                              |
| **Rumble / Roll** | Low-frequency, long-duration arrivals from distant or highly scattered channel segments |

#### Stochastic Tail Crackles
Few's "string of pearls" model also predicts discrete secondary arrivals from kinked micro-segments after the main crack — a Poisson process whose rate decays exponentially with time.

> **code:** `add_tail_crackles()` in `thunder_synth.cpp`. Rate and decay controlled by `CRACKLE_RATE_DEFAULT_HZ = 4.0` and `CRACKLE_DECAY_TAU_S = 4.0`.

#### $f^{-2}$ High-Frequency Rolloff
Few (1969) predicts that the spectrum above the peak frequency falls as $f^{-2}$ — a consequence of the cylindrical shock geometry. The 2nd-order Butterworth lowpass used in synthesis approximates this rolloff.

> **code:** `apply_butter2_lp()` in `thunder_synth.cpp`
> <br>LP cutoff at `lp_cutoff_factor × peak_hz` (default 1.5×).

#### 200 Hz Peak (Historical Context)
Few and Dessler cite a spectral peak near ~200 Hz for some recordings. This is now understood to reflect weak, low-current strikes (high $f_m$) measured close to the source. Typical observed dominant frequencies from natural lightning at normal listening distances are 30–100 Hz. See reconciliation under [3].

#### Infrasonic Thunder (Dessler contribution)
Audible thunder originates from rapid resistive heating of the air channel. **Infrasonic thunder** (0.2–2 Hz) has a separate origin: the sudden collapse of the cloud's electrostatic field following discharge, rather than acoustic emission from the heated channel. Not currently modelled.

## [2] Hong et al. (2023) — "Inversion of Acoustic Thunder Source Spectral Model from Thunder-Induced Seismic Waves in Megacity"
**Citation:** Hong, T.-K., Park, S., Chung, D. & Kim, B. (2023). *Inversion of acoustic thunder source spectral model from thunder-induced seismic waves in megacity.* Geophysical Journal International, 233(1), 107–126.
**DOI:** [10.1093/gji/ggac440](https://doi.org/10.1093/gji/ggac440)

**Context:** Analyses thunder-induced seismic records from 60 broadband stations in Seoul during COVID-19 lockdown (low ambient noise). Inverts back to acoustic thunder source spectra by removing propagation, atmospheric attenuation, and acoustic-to-seismic coupling effects. The seismology machinery (sections 4–9) is not relevant to synthesis — the useful outputs are the empirically-derived source and propagation parameters extracted below.

---

### Lightning Current → Peak Overpressure (Wang et al. 2019, via eq. 1)
$$P_{\text{max}} = 0.432 \, C_{\text{max}} - 0.19$$

where $C_{\text{max}}$ is lightning discharge current in kA and $P_{\text{max}}$ is peak overpressure in Pa.

**For typical natural lightning (100 kA):** $P_{\text{max}} \approx 43$ Pa at source.

Also from Wang et al.: peak frequency $f_{\text{max}}$ is **inversely proportional** to $C_{\text{max}}$ — stronger strikes produce lower-frequency, more bass-heavy thunder.

> **code:** `peak_overpressure_pa()` in `lightning_model.h`
> <br>Constants `WANG_PRESSURE_SLOPE_PA_PER_KA = 0.432` and `WANG_PRESSURE_OFFSET_PA = -0.19`.

---

### Source Pressure Model
A lightning acoustic source is modeled as an air explosion (Garces 2019; Kim et al. 2021):

$$P_0(t) = \begin{cases}
P_{\text{max}} \left(1 - \dfrac{t}{t_p}\right) & 0 \leq \dfrac{t}{t_p} \leq 1 \\[10pt]
\dfrac{P_{\text{max}}}{6} \left(1 - \dfrac{t}{t_p}\right)\!\left(1 + \sqrt{6} - \dfrac{t}{t_p}\right)^2 & 1 < \dfrac{t}{t_p} \leq 1 + \sqrt{6}
\end{cases}$$

| Symbol           | Meaning                                 |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| $P_0(t)$         | Sound pressure as a function of time    |
| $P_{\text{max}}$ | Peak overpressure                       |
| $t_p$            | Duration of the positive pressure pulse |

> **code:** `source_pulse()` in `lightning_model.h`
> <br>Pulse duration range: `CRACK_POSITIVE_PULSE_MIN_MS = 5.0` to `CRACK_POSITIVE_PULSE_MAX_MS = 20.0` — shorter for stronger current (sharper crack). Applied as the temporal envelope of the crack segment in `ThunderSynth::synthesize()`.

### Acoustic Particle Velocity at Source
$$A_0 = \frac{P_0}{Z} = \frac{P_0}{\rho \, V_{\text{air}}}$$
| Symbol                    | Meaning                     | Value               |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------- |
| $A_0$                     | Acoustic particle velocity  | m s⁻¹               |
| $Z = \rho V_{\text{air}}$ | Specific acoustic impedance | N·s m⁻³             |
| $\rho$                    | Air mass density            | 1.21 kg m⁻³         |
| $V_{\text{air}}$          | Speed of sound              | 344 m s⁻¹ (at 20°C) |

### Propagation and Attenuation
Acoustic waves propagate through the atmosphere with geometrical spreading and frequency-dependent absorption (Langston 2004):

$$\tilde{A}(r, f) = \frac{\tilde{A}_0}{r^n} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi f r}{V_{\text{air}} \, Q}\right)$$
| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $\tilde{A}(r, f)$ | Velocity spectral amplitude at distance $r$ (km) and frequency $f$ (Hz) |
| $\tilde{A}_0$ | Velocity spectral amplitude at the source |
| $n$ | Geometrical spreading exponent — see values below |
| $Q$ | Quality factor — controls attenuation from absorption and scattering |

> **code:** `PropagationFilter` in `propagation_model.h`
> <br>Applied per-segment via `PropagationFilter::apply()` in `thunder_synth.cpp`.

#### Geometrical Spreading Exponent $n$
| $n$ | Regime |
|-----|--------|
| 0.5 | Linear cylindrical wave — **correct for thunder from a distant lightning channel** |
| 0.75 | Weak shock wave — near-field / close strikes (nonlinear regime) |
| 1.0 | Spherical point-source approximation — overestimates attenuation for thunder |

For synthesis at typical listening distances (> ~1 km from strike), use $n = 0.5$.

> **code:** `PropagationConfig::spreading` enum (`Cylindrical` / `WeakShock` / `Spherical`)
> <br>Constants `SPREADING_N_CYLINDRICAL = 0.5`, `SPREADING_N_WEAK_SHOCK = 0.75`, `SPREADING_N_SPHERICAL = 1.0`.

#### Empirically Measured Quality Factors
Fit to measured Seoul thunder data across all three spreading assumptions:

$$Q(f) = \begin{cases} 2.9 \, f^{1.84} & n = 1.0 \\ 4.1 \, f^{1.69} & n = 0.75 \\ 5.2 \, f^{1.58} & n = 0.5 \end{cases}$$

For synthesis with cylindrical spreading ($n = 0.5$): use $Q(f) = 5.2 \, f^{1.58}$.

The paper finds that **atmospheric molecular absorption is relatively weak below 100 Hz** — scattering from local obstacles (buildings, terrain) dominates low-frequency attenuation. The large frequency exponent ($q \approx 1.58$) means high frequencies attenuate much more steeply than low with distance, explaining why distant thunder sounds purely bass-heavy.

For `openness` parameterization: higher openness → less scattering → raise $Q_0$ coefficient above 5.2; dense urban/forest → lower it.

> **code:** `THUNDER_Q0_CYLINDRICAL = 5.2`, `THUNDER_QEXP_CYLINDRICAL = 1.58`
> <br>`PropagationConfig::q_scatter_multiplier` scales $Q_0$ for environment type.

### Observed Spectral Characteristics
- **Dominant energy range:** 4–90 Hz (consistent across all four events)
- **Measured source peak frequencies:** ~34–36 Hz (inverted from seismic data, after removing propagation effects)
- **Literature range:** 20–130 Hz (Zhu & Stensrud), 25–55 Hz (Kappus & Vernon), 70–150 Hz (Lacroix et al.), <4–125 Hz (Holmes et al.) — wide variation driven by lightning current strength
- **Above 60 Hz:** attenuates rapidly with distance; rarely identifiable beyond ~10 km
- **Stronger current = lower peak frequency** (Wang et al. 2019)

> **Note:** The 34–36 Hz measured here is a **propagated** value at 2–4 km urban, not a source value. See [3] for reconciliation.

---

### Source Altitude and Geometry
Thunder sources were located at **1–4 km altitude** above the surface. This has two synthesis implications:

1. **Arrival angle:** Sound arrives from above at an oblique angle — relevant for directional spatial models
2. **Distance calculation:** Use 3D hypocentral distance (not horizontal ground range) in the propagation formula

> **code:** `LightningSegment::slant_distance_m` is the 3D hypocentral distance used by `PropagationFilter`.
> <br>`position_x/y/z_m` feeds `spatial::StereoProcessor`.

---

### Environmental Attenuation
**Urban landscapes cause additional attenuation** beyond atmospheric absorption alone — low-Q at low frequencies in Seoul data is attributed to high-rise buildings impeding transmission. Wind direction caused measurable azimuth-dependent spectral variation, not just amplitude roll-off.

> **code:** `EnvironmentProcessor` in `environment_model.h`
> <br>`EnvironmentConfig::openness` (0–1) scales FDN feedback and Q multiplier. `wind_direction_deg` drives HF directional roll-off.

## [3] Abeygunawardana et al. — "Frequency Analysis of Thunder Features"
**Authors:** Salath Abeygunawardana, JAP Bodhika, S Nanayakkara, Upul Sonnadara, M Fernando (University of Colombo, Sri Lanka); V Cooray (Uppsala University, Sweden)

### Method
Thunder recorded as calibrated pressure (100 mV/Pa conditioning amplifier), digitised at **100 kHz**. Time-frequency analysis via **S-transform** — a phase-corrected STFT with frequency-dependent time resolution, superior to standard STFT for non-stationary signals. Distinct features (peak, clap, rumble) isolated and analysed separately.

### Key Findings

#### Three-feature taxonomy (measured)
| Feature | Acoustic character | Frequency range |
|---------|--------------------|----------------|
| **Peak** | Point-source pressure pulse, sharp transient | 42–137 Hz; **>82% between 42–100 Hz** |
| **Clap** | Shock wave front + pressure fluctuations | Lower centroid than peak |
| **Rumble** | Extended, continuous | Low-frequency dominant |

All features have **fundamental frequencies below 230 Hz**. All content analysed is below 200 Hz.

> **code:** `THUNDER_PEAK_FREQ_MIN_HZ = 42.0`, `THUNDER_PEAK_FREQ_MAX_HZ = 137.0`, `THUNDER_PEAK_FREQ_BAND_LO/HI_HZ = 42/100`, `THUNDER_FUNDAMENTAL_CEIL_HZ = 230.0`
> <br>Used by `CrackAnalyzer::classify_feature()`.

#### Reconciling with Hong et al. (2023)
Hong et al. measured 34–36 Hz; this paper measures 42–100 Hz. The discrepancy is propagation: Hong et al. inverted from 2–4 km urban records where scattering (low Q) attenuates high frequencies and shifts the apparent peak downward. Near-field calibrated measurements are closer to the true source spectrum.

**Synthesis implication:** set source peak to ~65 Hz at 100 kA. Let `PropagationFilter` produce the distance-correct result — at 5 km urban it will shift to ~35–40 Hz, matching Hong et al.

#### Cloud flash vs. ground flash
Cloud flashes have longer duration than ground flashes due to greater propagation distance.

#### S-transform vs. STFT for crack analysis
The S-transform gives frequency-dependent time resolution: long windows at low frequencies (better frequency resolution), short windows at high frequencies (better temporal resolution). This is more accurate than a fixed-window STFT for the clap's low-frequency content.

> **code:** `CrackAnalyzerConfig::use_multiresolution` (default `true`) approximates this using `MultiResolutionSTFT` with three passes (LF/MF/HF bands at fft_size × 8 / × 2 / × 1)
> <br>This is an approximation of the S-transform, not a full implementation. Whether it's accurate enough for the 42–100 Hz band hasn't been formally compared.
